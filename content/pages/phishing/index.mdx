# Phishing

Phishing is a scam in which an attacker poses as something trustworthy to trick a person into
revealing a secret. In Bittensor the usual prize is a wallet's recovery material, most often the
seed phrase, which the documentation treats as sensitive wallet access material that should be kept
private.

References:
[Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase),
[Wallets, Coldkeys and Hotkeys](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/wallets)

## What It Targets

The center of a Bittensor phishing attempt is the recovery phrase that backs a wallet. The
documentation describes a seed phrase as material that can restore a wallet's keys, so an attacker
who obtains it can rebuild the wallet elsewhere and move its funds. A phishing attempt is, at heart,
an effort to get that phrase out of the owner's sole control by making a fake request look genuine.

Reference:
[Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase)

## Why the Seed Phrase Is the Prize

Phishing works for the same reason recovery phrases are powerful: the phrase is key-equivalent.
Because it can regenerate the keys it belongs to, disclosing it is close to handing over the wallet
itself. That is what makes a convincing fake prompt for a recovery phrase so dangerous, and it is
why the documentation frames disclosure of the phrase as a wallet-safety risk rather than a minor
mistake.

Reference:
[Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase)

## Reader Boundary

This page defines the concept at a high level. It does not catalog specific scams, sites, or
messages, and the forms phishing takes change constantly. The durable point is the target: a
wallet's recovery phrase is what a Bittensor phishing attempt is ultimately after, which is why that
phrase belongs nowhere but the owner's own secure keeping.

## Coldkey Recovery Phrases Are the Highest-Value Phishing Target

Official [Wallets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/wallets) documentation separates
[coldkeys](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#coldkey), which control TAO, stake,
and subnet ownership, from [hotkeys](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#hotkey),
which handle day-to-day subnet operations. A phishing attempt that captures coldkey recovery
material therefore threatens the wallet's highest-privilege signing role, not just a single
operational key.

That hierarchy explains attacker focus. Hotkey compromise can disrupt mining or validation activity,
but coldkey recovery material can regenerate the account that moves stake and holds long-term
custody. Phishing that asks for "your wallet words" is usually aiming at whichever recovery phrase
controls funds, and official guidance treats coldkey recovery as the most consequential case
([Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase)).

References: [Wallets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/wallets),
[Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase)

## Phishing Uses Out-of-Band Recovery Requests

Official [Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase)
guidance treats recovery words as material that should be generated and stored by the wallet owner,
not typed into unsolicited forms, chats, or support tickets. Phishing works by breaking that
boundary: a fake site, message, or "verification" step asks for recovery words outside the normal
local wallet creation or import flow.

The deception is contextual rather than cryptographic. The prompt may look like customer support, an
airdrop claim, or a wallet update, but the underlying request is the same: move recovery material
out of the owner's sole control. Recognizing that pattern — an unexpected out-of-band request for
recovery words — is the durable defense the documentation supports
([Wallets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/wallets)).

References:
[Handle your Seed Phrase Securely](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/handle-seed-phrase),
[Glossary: Mnemonic](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#mnemonic)

## Address Poisoning Targets Destinations, Not Secrets

[Address poisoning scams](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/address-poisoning-scams) and phishing
are both wallet-safety risks, but they exploit different mistakes. Address poisoning tries to make a
user sign toward a lookalike destination address, while phishing tries to extract recovery material
directly. One attack misroutes a value-bearing action; the other tries to copy the keys that could
authorize many actions.

Keeping that distinction visible helps readers classify an incident. A shortened address that almost
matches a prior transfer is address-poisoning vocabulary. A message asking to "confirm your seed
phrase" is phishing vocabulary. Both belong in wallet safety, yet the safe response differs: verify
the full destination in one case, and refuse to disclose recovery words in the other
([Address Poisoning Scams](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/address-poisoning-scams)).

References: [Address Poisoning Scams](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/address-poisoning-scams),
