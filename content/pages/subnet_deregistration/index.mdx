# Subnet Deregistration

Subnet deregistration is the Bittensor subnet-lifecycle mechanism that frees a subnet slot when the
network is full. The core case is simple: if the 128-slot cap is already occupied, adding another
subnet requires one eligible subnet to leave first
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration)).

## Why the Cap Matters

Bittensor treats subnet capacity as a bounded resource. The subnet cap keeps the network from
growing by unlimited slot creation, while the deregistration rule gives the system a way to rotate
capacity toward subnets with stronger market support
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration),
[Understanding Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/understanding-subnets)).

This makes deregistration part of subnet lifecycle vocabulary rather than a general failure label.
It is about freeing one subnet slot under a documented cap, not about judging every miner,
validator, or application running inside every subnet.

## Selection Rule

When the cap is full, the eligible subnet with the lowest exponential moving average price is the
removal candidate. EMA price is a smoothed
[alpha price](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha-price) signal, so the rule is
tied to market value over time rather than a one-moment price tick
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration)).

That distinction matters for interpretation. "Lowest EMA price" is the documented comparison, while
"weak subnet" is only shorthand. The mechanism does not require a manual content review, a social
vote, or a broad quality essay about the subnet.

## Immunity and Timing

Newer subnets can be protected by an
[immunity period](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#immunity-period), which keeps a
freshly created subnet out of the removal comparison while it has time to establish price history.
Deregistration is also paced by rate limits, so slot turnover belongs to the same family of delayed,
bounded network actions as other sensitive subnet lifecycle changes
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration),
[Rate Limits in Bittensor](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/chain-rate-limits)).

The practical reading is that selection is not just "lowest price wins removal." Eligibility,
immunity, and timing are part of the concept. A subnet can have a low market signal and still be
outside the comparison if the protected window applies.

## Creation Pressure

Subnet deregistration is coupled to subnet creation pressure. A full cap creates the need for slot
turnover; without that pressure, the concept is not just a standing ranking table. The lifecycle
pair is easier to read as "create a slot" and "free a slot" rather than as unrelated operations
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration),
[Create a Subnet](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/create-a-subnet)).

## Development Stage Context

The
[Introduction to Bittensor](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/introduction#subnet-development)
describes subnet development as moving from localnet to testnet and then mainnet. For subnet
deregistration, that sequence changes how readers should interpret cap-full examples, immunity
windows, and liquidation outcomes.

In localnet, deregistration mechanics can be tested in an isolated environment. Local lifecycle
events reflect local chain configuration rather than production subnet markets.

On testnet, deregistration behavior can be observed in a shared, non-production network. Testnet
subnet-cap and EMA-price readings are separate from mainnet subnet state.

On mainnet, subnet deregistration is the live mechanism that can remove an entire subnet market when
the 128-slot cap is full and a new subnet registration attempts to enter.

The [Bittensor Networks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/bittensor-networks) reference
separates mainnet, testnet, and localnet. A deregistration example from one environment should not
be read as representing production subnet lifecycle behavior in another environment.

## Subnet Registration Triggers Removal at Capacity

[Subnet registration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/create-a-subnet) creates a new subnet
by paying a registration burn to receive a unique netuid. When the subnet cap is already full, that
registration attempt can trigger subnet deregistration, selecting the eligible subnet with the
lowest EMA of alpha price for removal so the incoming subnet can take the slot.

The newly registered subnet then receives an immunity period, which protects it from being
immediately selected as a candidate for future subnet deregistration.

## Participant Deregistration Vacates One UID

[Participant deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#deregistration)
removes one miner or validator from a continuing subnet when registration pressure requires slot
turnover. Subnet deregistration removes the entire subnet context: every UID assignment within it
ends simultaneously rather than one participant at a time.

Subnet deregistration should be kept separate from miner or validator deregistration inside a subnet
that continues to exist.

## A Full Cap With All Subnets Immune Blocks Entry

When every occupied subnet is still inside its immunity window, the selection step returns no
removal candidate, registration fails with a subnet-limit error, and no subnet is deregistered until
at least one becomes eligible
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration)).

A full 128-slot network can still refuse a new subnet registration if every incumbent remains
immune.

## Liquidation Converts Alpha Back to TAO Holders

When a subnet is deregistered, alpha in the subnet is converted through the pool and distributed to
alpha holders, with the subnet's TAO reserve allocated pro-rata. Alpha tokens in that subnet are
destroyed as part of dissolution rather than continuing as a live subnet asset.

Deregistration therefore ends the subnet market and settles holder balances through the subnet
liquidity pool conversion path.

## Zero Emissions Do Not Automatically Remove a Subnet

[Emission](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/emissions) documentation notes that emissions and
deregistration are intentionally decoupled: subnets with zero flow-based injection can still remain
registered, while subnet deregistration continues to use lowest alpha-price EMA among eligible
subnets when the cap is full.

Negative or zero cross-subnet emission share does not, by itself, trigger the subnet-slot removal
rule described in deregistration documentation.

## Yuma Consensus Stops With the Subnet Market

Yuma Consensus aggregates validator weight signals within a subnet into miner incentives and
validator dividends. Subnet deregistration ends that subnet market entirely, so the consensus and
emission path for that netuid stops with the subnet context rather than continuing for individual
participants inside a live subnet
([Yuma Consensus](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/yuma-consensus)).

Subnet deregistration names market removal; Yuma Consensus names the incentive aggregation that
operated inside the market while it existed.

## Reader Boundary

Subnet deregistration removes a subnet slot when the 128-slot cap is full. It should be kept
separate from miner or validator deregistration inside a continuing subnet, from zero-emission
removal, and from predictions about future removals
([Subnet Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/subnet-deregistration),
[Glossary: Deregistration](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#deregistration)).

The documented comparison is lowest alpha-price EMA among eligible subnets, not a manual quality
review or social vote. Immunity windows, rate limits, and creation pressure are part of the concept,
not optional timing details.
