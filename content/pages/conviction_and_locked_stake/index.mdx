# Conviction and Locked Stake

Conviction and locked stake describe a Bittensor staking feature where subnet alpha stake can be
locked so that it builds a time-based commitment signal
([Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Staking and delegation overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/delegation)).

The feature is commitment vocabulary. It does not create a separate token, a separate subnet role,
or a general staking recommendation.

## Locked Alpha Stake

Locked stake is subnet alpha stake constrained by a lock. The conviction staking documentation
explains that the locked amount limits how far the staked alpha balance on that subnet can fall
while the lock is active
([Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Glossary: Alpha Tokens](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha-tokens)).

That makes locked stake narrower than ordinary stake. The stake still belongs to a subnet alpha
context, but the locked portion carries an additional commitment constraint.

The subnet context should stay visible whenever the term is used. Conviction is tied to locked alpha
in a subnet position, not to every staking balance in Bittensor.

## Conviction Score

Conviction is the score that grows from locked stake over time. The locked amount and the passage of
time both matter, so conviction should be read as a maturing commitment signal rather than a simple
balance number
([Conviction score](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#the-conviction-score),
[Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking)).

This keeps conviction separate from stake itself. Stake names the amount associated with a staking
position; conviction names the time-based signal built from locked stake.

## Lock Modes

The conviction model includes decaying and perpetual modes. Decaying mode reduces the locked amount
over time, while perpetual mode keeps the locked amount from decaying until the mode changes
([Decaying and perpetual modes](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#decaying-and-perpetual-modes),
[Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking)).

The mode affects how the commitment signal evolves. It should not be treated as a separate asset
type or as a promise about validator rewards.

## Subnet and Stake Context

Conviction and locked stake apply to subnet alpha already staked inside a specific subnet. Alpha
tokens are subnet-specific, and staking into subnets places stake in subnet-local context
([Glossary: Alpha Tokens](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha-tokens),
[Staking into Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/delegation#staking-into-subnets)).

The concept is therefore separate from Root Subnet TAO staking and from ordinary unlocked stake. An
alpha position can be staked without being locked; conviction begins when part of that staked alpha
is locked for commitment signaling.

## Movement Boundary

Locked stake should be read as a movement constraint on an existing subnet alpha staking position,
not as a new wallet or a separate token balance. The conviction-staking documentation describes the
lock in terms of how far the staked alpha balance can fall, while subnet staking keeps the position
inside subnet-local alpha context
([Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Staking into Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/delegation#staking-into-subnets)).

That distinction keeps the commitment signal separate from custody language. The lock constrains
movement of the staked alpha position; it does not rename the asset or make conviction a wallet
authority term.

## Environment Scope

Bittensor separates localnet, testnet, and mainnet environments. Conviction examples from one
environment should not be treated as evidence for another because lock state, alpha balances, and
subnet conditions are environment-specific
([Bittensor Networks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/bittensor-networks),
[Introduction to Bittensor: Subnet development](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/introduction#subnet-development)).

Localnet examples are isolated development examples. Testnet examples are shared non-production
examples. Mainnet conviction and locked stake concern production subnet alpha and production subnet
state.

## Conviction Score Reflects Duration and Amount Together

The
[conviction score documentation](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#the-conviction-score)
describes conviction as a function of both the locked alpha amount and the duration for which that
amount has been locked. Neither input determines the score in isolation: a large locked amount held
for a short period produces a lower conviction score than a moderate locked amount held for a
significantly longer period, and a long lock duration on a small locked amount can still yield a
meaningful conviction score. The two inputs combine into a single score that the documentation
labels a time-based commitment signal, where time is as load-bearing as the locked amount itself.
This combined structure is why the term conviction score appears alongside locked stake rather than
simply as a synonym for staked alpha: the staked amount is only one side of what the score measures
([Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Conviction score](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#the-conviction-score),
[Glossary: Alpha Tokens](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha-tokens)).

That dual-input structure means identical conviction scores can arise from different underlying
combinations of amount and duration. A validator holding a small locked alpha position that has been
continuously locked for a long period may have the same conviction score as a validator holding a
larger locked position that was locked more recently. Readers interpreting conviction scores should
therefore not treat a high score as evidence of a large locked balance, or a low score as evidence
of a short commitment period, without knowing which combination of inputs produced the result. The
conviction score compresses both dimensions into a single number, and unpacking the score requires
looking at both the locked amount and the lock duration separately rather than relying on the
combined score alone to reconstruct either dimension
([Conviction score](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#the-conviction-score),
[Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Glossary: Alpha Tokens](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha-tokens)).

Conviction score is a commitment signal, not a consensus influence measure. It describes the
maturity of a staking commitment rather than the validator's evaluation power inside Yuma Consensus.
Stake weight, which combines alpha stake and TAO stake within the subnet consensus framework, is the
figure that describes validator influence over subnet outcomes
([Glossary: Stake Weight](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#stake-weight),
[Yuma Consensus](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/yuma-consensus)). Conviction score and stake
weight serve different purposes: stake weight answers how strongly a validator's evaluation signal
counts in subnet consensus, while conviction score answers how mature the validator's locked staking
commitment is relative to the duration and size of the lock. A high conviction score does not
translate into higher stake weight or greater influence in subnet consensus; the two measures belong
to different parts of Bittensor vocabulary. Treating conviction score as a proxy for consensus power
would conflate commitment vocabulary with consensus-influence vocabulary, which are addressed by
separate terms in the Bittensor staking model
([Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking),
[Glossary: Stake Weight](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#stake-weight),
[Yuma Consensus](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/yuma-consensus)).

The two lock modes affect the amount and duration inputs differently. In decaying mode, the locked
amount decreases over time, so the amount dimension of the conviction score falls as decay proceeds
even while the duration dimension continues to accumulate
([Decaying and perpetual modes](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#decaying-and-perpetual-modes)).
In perpetual mode, the locked amount is held constant rather than allowed to decay, so the amount
dimension stays fixed while the duration dimension grows uninterrupted. A validator in perpetual
mode therefore sees conviction grow primarily through the duration channel, while a validator in
decaying mode sees the amount channel shrink over time, potentially offsetting some of the
conviction that the duration channel adds. Choosing between modes determines which of the two inputs
remains stable and which one changes rather than choosing between two entirely different commitment
mechanisms, because the underlying dual-input structure of conviction score applies in both modes.
Mode switching changes the trajectory of the amount dimension but does not change the fact that both
amount and duration contribute to the conviction score throughout the lock period
([Decaying and perpetual modes](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking#decaying-and-perpetual-modes),
[Conviction and locked stake](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/staking-and-delegation/conviction-staking)).
