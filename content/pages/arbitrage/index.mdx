# Arbitrage

Arbitrage is profiting from a price difference for the same thing in two places, by buying where it
is cheaper and selling where it is dearer. In Bittensor it appears because each subnet's alpha
trades in its own pool, so an alpha price can drift apart from how the broader market values it, and
traders can act on the gap
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).

## Where the Gaps Come From

A subnet's alpha price is set inside its own automated market maker pool, by the ratio of TAO to
alpha held in reserve. Because each subnet prices its token separately, the same kind of asset can
sit at different effective prices across subnets, or differ between a subnet's on-chain pool and an
outside market. Those differences are the openings an arbitrageur looks for
([Understanding Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/understanding-subnets)).

## How It Works Here

The documentation notes that liquidity provision creates opportunities for arbitrageurs to profit
from price discrepancies across subnets or between on-chain and off-chain markets. An arbitrageur
buys the underpriced side and sells the overpriced one, pocketing the gap. The trade is ordinary
swapping; what makes it arbitrage is that it targets a mismatch rather than a directional bet on
where a price will go next
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).

## Why It Helps Keep Prices Honest

Arbitrage is not only extraction. Buying the cheap side and selling the dear side pushes the two
prices toward each other, so the act of capturing a gap also closes it. In aggregate,
self-interested arbitrage keeps a subnet's pool price tracking the broader market rather than
drifting freely, which is part of why deeper, more liquid pools tend to show tighter, more
consistent prices ([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions),
[Understanding Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/understanding-subnets)).

## On-Chain and Off-Chain Markets Can Diverge

[User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions) documentation
describes arbitrage opportunities from price discrepancies across subnets or between on-chain and
off-chain markets. Each subnet prices
[alpha](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha) inside its own automated market
maker pool, so the same economic exposure can trade at different effective rates in another subnet
pool or outside the chain.

Arbitrage vocabulary therefore covers cross-venue gaps, not only two swaps inside one pool. A trader
closes the mismatch by buying the cheaper side and selling the dearer one until the spread no longer
covers transaction costs.

## Slippage Sets the Cost to Close a Gap

[Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#slippage) is the difference between
expected and received amounts when a trade moves through subnet pool reserves. Each arbitrage swap
pushes the pool ratio and pays that movement cost, so a visible price gap is worth acting on only
while expected profit exceeds slippage and fees
([Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage)).

Thin pools can show larger gaps but punish size with heavier slippage, while deeper pools tend to
show smaller gaps that are cheaper to trade away. Arbitrage therefore competes with the same pool
mechanics that create the opportunity.

## Price Discovery Depends on Independent Trades

[Price discovery](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#price-discovery) names how a
subnet's alpha price emerges from independent buying and selling in the pool. Arbitrage acts on
temporary mispricings by routing trades toward alignment: buying the underpriced side and selling
the overpriced one narrows the gap that attracted the trade
([Understanding Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/understanding-subnets)).

That role is separate from adversarial transaction ordering.
[MEV](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#mev-maximal-extractable-value) vocabulary
concerns value extracted by reordering or observing pending transactions, while arbitrage concerns
profiting from price differences that already exist between venues or pools.

## AMM Pools, Slippage, and Liquidity Routing Pipeline

Arbitrage in Bittensor follows the same reserve-based path as any subnet swap. The useful pipeline
order on a subnet such as netuid 1 is an independent per-subnet automated market maker pool, a
discovered alpha price from the TAO-to-alpha reserve ratio, a visible price gap across subnets or
against an off-chain venue, and a routing trade that buys the cheaper side and sells the dearer one
until slippage and fees consume the remaining spread
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions),
[Understanding Subnets](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/subnets/understanding-subnets)).

Each subnet prices [alpha](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#alpha) inside its own
pool, so arbitrage vocabulary covers cross-venue gaps rather than only two swaps inside one reserve
pair. A trader closes the mismatch by routing liquidity toward the underpriced side and away from
the overpriced one
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).

[Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#slippage) sets the cost of that
routing step. Every arbitrage swap moves pool reserves and pays the difference between expected and
received amounts, so a gap is worth closing only while expected profit exceeds slippage and fees
([Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage)).

Thin pools on netuid 1 can show larger gaps but punish size with heavier slippage, while deeper
pools tend to show smaller gaps that are cheaper to trade away. Liquidity depth, slippage cost, and
arbitrage opportunity therefore move together inside the same AMM mechanics
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).

[Price discovery](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#price-discovery) names the
emergent pool price that arbitrage pushes back toward alignment. Self-interested gap closing is not
the same as adversarial transaction ordering:
[MEV](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/resources/glossary#mev-maximal-extractable-value) vocabulary
concerns value extracted by reordering or observing pending transactions, while arbitrage concerns
profiting from price differences that already exist between venues or pools.

## Development Stage Context

The
[Introduction to Bittensor](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/introduction#subnet-development)
describes subnet development as moving from localnet to testnet and then mainnet. For arbitrage on a
subnet such as netuid 1, that sequence changes how readers should interpret pool-price gaps,
slippage costs, and cross-venue spread examples
([Bittensor Networks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/bittensor-networks)).

In localnet, arbitrage mechanics can be tested in an isolated environment. Localnet pool depth and
price gaps on netuid 1 do not represent production market conditions.

On testnet, arbitrage flows can be exercised in a shared non-production network. Testnet pool prices
and liquidity on netuid 1 are separate from mainnet subnet state.

On mainnet, arbitrage concerns live production subnet pools and any off-chain venues traders compare
against on-chain alpha prices. Observed spreads on netuid 1 depend on the selected subnet's pool
reserves, liquidity, and trading costs at the time of the trade
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).

The [Bittensor Networks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/bittensor-networks) reference
separates mainnet, testnet, and localnet. An arbitrage example from one environment should not be
read as representing production price behavior in another environment.

## Reader Boundary

This page defines the concept at a high level and is not trading advice. Whether any gap exists, and
whether acting on it is profitable after costs, depends on live prices and liquidity that change
continuously. The durable point is the role arbitrage plays: turning price differences into a force
that pulls pool prices back into line
([User Liquidity Positions](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/liquidity-positions)).
