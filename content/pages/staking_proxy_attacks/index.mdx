# Staking Proxy Attacks

Staking proxy attacks are a Bittensor wallet-safety concept about misused delegated staking
authority. The official
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
page frames the issue around a key boundary: a staking proxy is not a direct transfer key, but its
authority can still matter if the delegate is compromised.

This makes the topic narrower than full wallet compromise. It is about the risk left inside a
permission that is intentionally limited to staking-related activity.

Reference:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)

## Delegated Staking Authority

The official [Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies) explains proxy
relationships as scoped delegation between accounts. In that model, a staking proxy has less
authority than a wallet with full control, but it still has access to staking-related actions for
the protected account.

That distinction is the core of staking proxy attack risk. Reduced authority is not the same thing
as no authority.

References: [Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies),
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)

## Value-Loss Context

The official page connects staking proxy compromise to unfavorable staking and unstaking outcomes
rather than to a simple direct transfer. That places the concept near Bittensor's pool-conversion
topics.

[Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage) explains why staking and
unstaking can produce received amounts that differ from a static expectation. Staking proxy attacks
are a safety context where delegated staking authority intersects with that kind of outcome risk.

References:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks),
[Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage)

## Relation to Nearby Protections

Staking proxy attacks are adjacent to MEV Shield and price protection, but they are not the same
concept. [MEV Shield](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/mev-shield/) concerns pre-inclusion
transaction visibility. [Price Protection](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/price-protection)
concerns adverse price-movement handling around staking-related actions.

Staking proxy attacks name the delegated-authority risk. MEV Shield and price protection describe
other risk controls in nearby transaction and staking contexts.

References: [MEV Shield](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/mev-shield/),
[Price Protection](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/price-protection)

## What a Compromised Staking Proxy Can Still Do

A staking proxy does not receive full wallet authority, but compromise is still meaningful. The
official
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
page explains that an attacker with a compromised staking proxy can perform staking-related actions
on the protected account. Those actions can include unfavorable staking or unstaking patterns that
change how the account's stake is positioned, rather than sending TAO directly to an unrelated
address.

That outcome is why the concept sits next to slippage and pool-conversion topics. Staking and
unstaking can produce amounts that differ from a static quote, and a malicious actor acting through
a staking proxy can move the protected account through those operations. The harm is value
positioning and loss through adverse conversions, not necessarily a clean transfer drain.

Readers should therefore treat limited authority as a reduction in risk, not as proof the protected
wallet is safe while the proxy is exposed.

## Practical Risk Reduction

The official guidance frames staking proxy attacks as preventable through how proxy authority is
granted and monitored. Proxies are a normal Bittensor pattern when their scope is understood, but a
staking proxy should be paired with operational habits that match its authority: knowing which
account can act, what staking actions it can trigger, and whether announcements or delays are in use
for sensitive proxy types where applicable.

The useful reader takeaway is separation of roles. The protected coldkey remains the custody anchor,
the proxy account performs scoped work, and compromise of the proxy account becomes a
staking-activity problem rather than an immediate full-wallet transfer problem — but only if the
proxy type and surrounding controls were chosen with that boundary in mind.

References:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks),
[Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies),

## How This Differs From Transfer Compromise

Staking proxy attacks are intentionally narrower than coldkey compromise or a proxy with transfer
authority. A coldkey loss threatens balances, ownership, and hotkey management. A transfer-capable
proxy can move funds according to its permissions. A staking proxy, by contrast, exposes the
protected account mainly to staking-path manipulation.

MEV Shield and price protection address adjacent transaction and conversion risks, but they do not
replace careful proxy design. Staking proxy attacks name delegated-authority misuse; the other tools
address visibility and price-movement handling in nearby contexts. All three can matter to a wallet
strategy, but they solve different parts of the problem.

## Reading Boundary

The term should not be read as a claim that proxy wallets are inherently unsafe. Proxy wallets are a
normal Bittensor security pattern when their authority is understood. Staking proxy attacks describe
the narrower case where delegated staking authority is misused or exposed.

## Relationship to Proxy Wallets

Staking proxy attacks and proxy wallets are related but different parts of Bittensor wallet-security
vocabulary. Proxy wallets are the normal security pattern where one account acts for a protected
wallet with limited authority, keeping sensitive keys offline while allowing delegated operations.
Staking proxy attacks describe the risk case where that delegated staking authority is compromised
and misused, even though it lacks full transfer authority. The
[Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies) describes proxy wallets as scoped
delegation for operational safety, while
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
explains the residual risk when a staking proxy is compromised.

For readers, proxy wallets name the security pattern itself, while staking proxy attacks name the
specific compromise scenario where delegated staking authority becomes a value-positioning risk.

References: [Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies),
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)

## Relationship to Slippage

Staking proxy attacks and slippage are related but different parts of Bittensor transaction-risk
vocabulary. Slippage describes the difference between expected and received amounts when staking or
unstaking due to pool conversion mechanics, while staking proxy attacks describe the wallet-safety
risk where a compromised staking proxy can perform unfavorable staking operations that expose the
protected account to adverse conversion outcomes. The
[Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage) documentation explains why
received amounts can differ from static expectations during pool operations, and
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
frames staking proxy compromise as enabling value loss through adverse staking patterns.

For readers, slippage names the conversion-outcome variability that can occur in any staking
transaction, while staking proxy attacks name the security scenario where compromised delegated
authority allows an attacker to move the protected wallet through those operations maliciously.

References: [Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage),
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
