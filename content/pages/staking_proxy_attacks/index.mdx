# Staking Proxy Attacks

Staking proxy attacks are a Bittensor wallet-safety risk involving delegated staking authority. The
official
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)
guide explains that a leaked staking proxy key can still create value-loss risk even though it is
not a direct transfer key.

The concept matters because proxy permissions reduce authority, but they do not make a compromised
delegate harmless. A staking proxy can still affect staking and unstaking behavior, so it belongs in
the same safety conversation as proxy-wallet scope, slippage, MEV exposure, and price protection.

Reference:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)

## Why Limited Authority Still Matters

Proxy wallets let one account act for another with a defined permission scope. The official
[Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies) presents this as a way to keep a
more sensitive wallet in a safer environment while a delegated account performs narrower work.

That narrower scope is still real authority. A staking proxy is not expected to move funds through
ordinary transfers, but it can influence staking-related actions. The attack risk comes from
treating that delegated staking authority as low-impact simply because it is not full wallet
control.

References: [Proxies overview](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies),
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks)

## Delay and Monitoring

The official staking-proxy attack guide emphasizes non-zero delay and monitoring as the important
boundary. A delayed proxy creates a waiting period before a delegated action can execute, while a
zero-delay proxy provides no review window. The same guide also warns that delay only helps when the
protected account owner actually monitors pending announcements before the delay expires.

For readers, the concept is not merely whether a proxy exists. The important question is whether the
delegated authority is scoped, delayed, and monitored well enough that unexpected staking activity
can be noticed and rejected.

References:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks),
[Working with Proxies](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/keys/proxies/working-with-proxies)

## Slippage Context

The harm described by the official guide is tied to unfavorable staking and unstaking outcomes, not
to a simple one-step transfer. Bittensor's
[Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage) documentation explains that
staking and unstaking can produce received amounts that differ from a static expectation because
subnet pool conditions matter.

This is why staking proxy attacks sit near slippage as a safety topic. Slippage is the pool outcome
concept; a compromised staking proxy is one way delegated authority can be misused around that
outcome.

References:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks),
[Understanding Slippage](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/slippage)

## Relationship to MEV Shield and Price Protection

MEV Shield and price protection address related but separate risk layers. The official staking-proxy
attack guide notes that the risk can unfold across multiple delegated actions, so encrypted mempool
protection should not be treated as the whole answer. The
[MEV Shield](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/mev-shield/) docs describe protection for
pre-inclusion transaction visibility, while
[Price Protection](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/price-protection) describes guardrails for
adverse price movement during staking-related actions.

For readers, this keeps the boundary clear. MEV Shield addresses pending-transaction visibility.
Price protection addresses adverse price movement. Staking proxy attack risk is about what a
compromised staking delegate can cause when delegated authority is not delayed, monitored, and
handled carefully.

References:
[Avoid Staking Proxy Attacks](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/avoid-staking-proxy-attacks),
[MEV Shield](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/concepts/mev-shield/),
[Price Protection](https://docs.learnbittensor.org/learn/price-protection)

## Security Boundaries

A staking proxy should be treated as a meaningful operational permission. Its scope is narrower than
full wallet control, but that does not make it harmless. The official guide's practical message is
that staking delegates need careful scope, delay, monitoring, and revocation habits.

The safe article-level reading is narrow: staking proxy attacks are a wallet-safety risk around
misused delegated staking authority. They are not a claim that every staking proxy is unsafe, nor a
replacement for understanding the broader proxy-wallet model.
